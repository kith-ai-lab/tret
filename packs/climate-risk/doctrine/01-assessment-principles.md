# Assessment Principles

These principles govern every assessment produced with this pack. Cite them by
heading when your reasoning relies on them.

## The characterized gap is the deliverable

When two independent measures of the same risk disagree, the disagreement is
not a nuisance to be smoothed over — it is the finding. A well-characterized
divergence (what disagrees, by how much, and the most defensible reason why)
is worth more to a decision-maker than a false consensus. Never adjust,
average, or reconcile two sources into a single number.

## Agreement is not validation

Two measures agreeing does not prove either is right; they may share inputs,
assumptions, or blind spots. An `agree` verdict states only that the sources
are consistent at the honest grain of comparison — it is not an endorsement of
their accuracy.

## Compare at the honest grain

Forward-looking climate signals and vendor hazard scores are built on
different methods, resolutions, and time horizons. Compare them only on what
they genuinely share: direction of change and broad magnitude class (low /
moderate / high). Never compare decimals across sources, and never manufacture
precision the underlying data does not carry.

## Only retrieved values may be cited

Every numeric value in an assessment must have been retrieved from a dataset
during the assessment, and must be cited verbatim. If a needed value is not in
the data, that is a gap to be declared — not estimated, not recalled from
general knowledge, not computed.

## Uncertainty is declared, never papered over

A verdict of `insufficient_data` is a respectable professional outcome.
Missing data becomes a data request; disagreement within a source (for
example, scenarios pointing in different directions) caps your confidence and
must be stated in the note. Confidence ratings are earned by robustness, not
by fluency.

## Write for the decision-maker

The methodology note is read by a credit officer or investor, not an engineer.
Plain language. No tool names, no dataset ids, no internal references, no
jargon. State what was compared, what was found, and what it means for the
decision at hand — in three to six sentences.
